## Bottom line

1. **No rule says "follow the most cautious official product."** ICAO Doc 9974 and the EASA guidance copied from it make the **operator** responsible. As a minimum the operator should monitor the VAAC advisory (VAA/VAG), SIGMETs and NOTAMs. It should also plan with information from other sources, such as pilot reports, satellite imagery and State or commercial information, that "it considers accurate and relevant". The guidance explicitly expects the operator to **"carefully consider and resolve differences or conflicts among the information sources."** When an eruption starts and the picture is uncertain, the default action is to re-route around the affected airspace. The UK adds that, for UK-regulated operators, where no ash concentration is published (true for Darwin and Tokyo VAAC areas as of this writing), the whole affected airspace counts as "high" contamination. [verified 2026-10-10: this is ICAO/EASA guidance ("should"), not a hard "must"; the UK "treated as forecast high" rule binds UK AOC holders only; QVA (quantitative volcanic ash) became applicable on 27 Nov 2025 and is recommended at all VAACs from 26 Nov 2026, so the "no concentration levels" situation for Darwin and Tokyo may change soon. See section 1 and the verification log.] [verified 2026-10-10, second pass: the UK rule is the current UK rule but is under review. UK AIC P 157/2025 says the Met Office/Météo-France medium/high charts it refers to retire on 27 Nov 2026 and that "the CAA will be reviewing the applicable material contained in CAP1236". Do not present it as a rule for Asia-Pacific VAAC areas.]
2. **Who decides.** The airline decides, through its safety risk assessment (SRA). The Australian carriers' public statements name "our meteorologists and operations teams" (Jetstar; the same wording is used by Qantas Group for Qantas, which suggests one shared group met and OCC function) and "our team of expert meteorologists" (Virgin Australia). Dispatchers and flight planners carry the decisions out under written procedures. ICAO expressly allows airlines to buy this expertise from third parties.
3. **What the vendor tools show.** Lido Weather (a pilot-facing EFB module, iPadOS/Windows, Lido mPilot) draws SIGMET and Volcanic Ash Advisory polygons on its enroute map. On the dispatch side, Lido Flight 4D builds volcanic ash into flight planning through its Natural Hazard Mitigation (NHM) feature and raises generic alerts when disruption affects "designated flights" (the page calls Lido Flight WINDS a legacy module replaced by Lido Flight 4D IFS). Weathernews sells its own ash dispersion forecasts, overlays them on routes alongside SIGMETs, and (for turbulence, co-developed with JAL) lists the aircraft affected and uplinks warnings to them by ACARS. None of the vendor pages I checked mentions VONAs or satellite ash retrievals, or says it flags disagreements between sources. [verified 2026-10-10: corrected; earlier text implied the polygons were a dispatch-tool feature and that Lido had no ash handling beyond map overlays.]
4. **Pain the evidence supports:** (a) uncertainty and delay in the first hours: "A reasonably detailed prediction will usually be available only after the eruption" (EASA). (b) Airlines reach different decisions from the same official information (Qantas and Virgin "at odds over whether it was safe to fly" in June 2011; Qantas delaying while Jetstar and Virgin cancelled on 7 July 2025), and an airport has said its own airspace was clear while at least 24 international flights were cancelled. (c) Large costs: about US$1.7 bn in 2010, and an estimated A$21m (Qantas) and A$11m (Virgin) in 2011 (Macquarie Equities analysts' estimate, not company figures). **Pain the evidence does not support:** dispatchers saying in their own words that they struggle. I found no such account, and a second, longer search also found none. The nearest evidence is Mulder et al. (2017), now read in full: airline operations respondents (including flight planners) said "there are so many operational decisions to be made in a short time during a volcanic eruption" and worried that digesting uncertainty information "would take too much time". Shown conflicting forecasts, respondents became more conservative overall but were "more risk-seeking with high-risk flight paths and more risk-averse with low-risk flight paths", while in discussion they said they would "only ever increase" their no-fly zones. The authors call that gap an "inaccurate perception of the process amongst users". This is researcher-reported time pressure and poorly calibrated choices under conflict, not a complaint of struggling. [verified 2026-10-10: Mulder et al. moved from REPORTED to CONFIRMED; open access at https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/68953/.]
5. **Akash's wording.** He is right to drop "dispatchers are struggling." "Dispatchers must act on the cautious one" is not what the rules say, and "often differ" has no measured frequency behind it. A stronger and accurate line is in the section "Pitch wording".

**Answer to question 1.** The rules give the airline discretion, and they require it to resolve conflicts between sources. They do not say "take the most cautious product." In practice, in the Asia-Pacific:
- VAAC Darwin and VAAC Tokyo publish no concentration levels (as of this writing; QVA is recommended at all VAACs for significant ash clouds from 26 Nov 2026, and a July 2026 Asia-Pacific VAAC report says Darwin is moving its ensemble tooling toward QVA [REPORTED: search summary of MET SG/30; PDF returned 403]).
- The UK rule treats any such area as "high" (for UK-regulated operators only).
- EASA's guidance makes avoidance the default at the onset of an eruption.

So the official area works as a no-go zone. The airline's real discretion lies in buffers, flight levels (above or below the ash), timing, delay versus cancellation, and how much weight to give the non-VAAC information. Airlines do use that discretion differently (see section 4).

## 2. Who in the airline does the ash assessment?

- **The operator as an organisation, under its SRA**, not an individual dispatcher. The CAA must accept the SRA, and the guidance requires "sufficient qualified and competent staff to generate well-supported operational risk management decisions." Doc 9974 adds: "It is not intended that the operator be precluded from securing necessary resources from other competent parties." (2.5 d) [CONFIRMED: copac.es copy, accessed 2026-10-10]
- **In-house meteorologists plus the operations centre, at the Australian carriers.**
  - Jetstar, July 2025: "our meteorologists and operations teams are monitoring the situation closely."
  - Virgin Australia, same event: "Our team of expert meteorologists is closely monitoring conditions and ash cloud activity."

  [CONFIRMED: https://thenightly.com.au/australia/bali-flights-cancelled-as-mount-lewotobi-laki-laki-eruption-causes-holiday-chaos-c-19283750, published 2025-07-08, accessed 2026-10-10] [verified 2026-10-10: the eruption and first cancellations were on Monday 7 July 2025; these quotes were published on 8 July (Virgin says "yesterday"; Jetstar was talking about Tuesday operations). The "meteorologists and operations teams" wording is Qantas Group wording, also used for Qantas in ABC News, 7 Jul 2025: "Our meteorologists and operations teams are closely monitoring the movement of volcanic ash" [REPORTED: ABC News, 7 Jul 2025, read by verifier; URL not recorded].]
  - Virgin Australia, March 2025: "Virgin Australia's meteorologists are closely monitoring ash cloud movement in Indonesia." [CONFIRMED: https://www.broomead.com.au/news/bali/flights-cancelled-in-and-out-of-bali-due-to-volcanic-ash-c-18111531, published 2025-03-20/21, accessed 2026-10-10]
- **Dispatchers and flight planners** execute the decisions. They are named in the procedures (Doc 9974 2.5 b; EASA GM (b)), and EASA's first action, "determine if any aircraft in flight could be affected, alert the crew", is flight-watch work. In the US, dispatchers share operational control: the ADF describes the dispatcher as "The only certificated aviation professional sharing operational control with the pilot-in-command." [CONFIRMED: https://www.dispatcher.org/, accessed 2026-10-10]
- **Vendors and met services can supply the analysis:** MetService on request (above); Weathernews sells ash dispersion forecasts to airlines (section 3).
- **Final authority in flight rests with the captain.** IFALPA: "the ultimate responsibility for the safe conduct of a flight rests with the Pilot-In-Command." [CONFIRMED: https://www.ifalpa.org/media/2105/12pos01-operations-in-the-presence-of-volcanic-contamination.pdf, accessed 2026-10-10]

Implication for TEPHRA: the user is the **OCC duty manager or duty meteorologist plus the dispatcher**, not a dispatcher working alone. The phone approval should come from whoever the airline's SRA names. The pitch should say "duty dispatcher or OCC duty manager."

## 4. First-hand accounts and what airlines actually did

- **Lewotobi, 7 July 2025.** Virgin Australia and Jetstar cancelled flights. "By Monday afternoon, at least 24 flights between Bali and Australia, Singapore and South Korea were cancelled ... while flights on four domestic routes were cancelled, said Ahmad Syaugi Shahab, spokesperson for Bali's Ngurah Rai International Airport." The same spokesperson said the airport "was running normally despite the cancellations, as monitoring showed the volcanic ash had not affected Bali's airspace." Jetstar: "Volcanic ash ... is continuing to move west and away from Bali", and it expected normal operations, while the same article reports cancellations "for a second day running". ## Implications for TEPHRA (short)

1. **The regulator already describes TEPHRA's job.** EASA GM (2013), copied from Doc 9974, lists the first actions after an eruption: find which aircraft in flight could be affected, alert the crew, re-plan departures, gather all relevant data, and resolve conflicts between published information and observations. Put this quote on a slide. It answers "is this a real task?" without claiming anyone is struggling.
2. **"Never shrink the official polygon" fits the rules and practice.** UK CAP 1236 makes VAAC and NAA information "primary", and (for UK operators, until the CAA's QVA review) treats areas without concentration data as "high". TEPHRA's rule matches that. An airline operations respondent in Mulder et al. (2017) describes the same rule in practice: when official and proprietary forecasts differ, "they would only ever increase their no-fly zones, never decrease them." Its added value is in what the rules leave to the operator: other sources, conflicts, timing, and the per-flight action. CAP 1236 also ties the SRA to "the capability of the operations unit to monitor volcanic ash information", and EASA GM's "Pre-eruption" item asks operators to define a strategy for catching increased activity before IAVW alerts ("the 60% of volcanoes which are unmonitored"). Both are regulatory hooks for TEPHRA.
3. **Do not claim novelty for "which flights are affected", and do not say incumbents ignore ash.** Lido Flight 4D alerts on designated flights and builds volcanic ash into flight planning (Natural Hazard Mitigation). Weathernews builds affected-aircraft lists with ACARS uplink (turbulence, with JAL) and sells its own ash dispersion forecasts; it is the closest competitor pattern. The pages checked do not advertise using VONAs, labelling each disagreement as lag, envelope or real conflict, or an approval step.
4. **Plan for QVA.** From 26 Nov 2026 all VAACs are recommended to issue quantitative, probabilistic ash data for significant clouds. That adds one more product to reconcile, which strengthens TEPHRA's reconciliation story, but it may also make "Darwin and Tokyo publish no concentration levels" out of date soon after the 18 Oct deadline. Phrase it as "until QVA reaches the Asia-Pacific". UK AIC P 157/2025 gives a dated hook: the UK medium/high charts retire on 27 Nov 2026, and operators must be able to "extract and visualise QVA forecast data" (IWXXM and NetCDF) and brief "operations control staff". It is UK-only, so use it as evidence of where regulators are heading, not as an Asia-Pacific requirement.
6. **A sharper pain line than "dispatchers struggle".** Mulder et al. (2017) found that users say they widen no-fly zones under conflict, but their measured choices under conflicting forecasts were poorly calibrated (riskier on high-risk paths, more cautious on low-risk ones). A tool that shows every disagreement and never shrinks the official area addresses exactly that gap. Quote it as a 2017 UK study of 25 respondents, not as Asia-Pacific evidence.
5. **The user is the OCC, not the dispatcher alone.** Name the approver as "duty dispatcher / OCC duty manager". Show the airline's meteorologist as a reader of the reconciliation view.

## Pitch wording

**Akash's proposal:** "the official sources often differ, and dispatchers must act on the cautious one quickly."
- "official sources ... differ": supported. Doc 9974 and EASA GM explicitly anticipate "conflicts among data sources", and IFALPA documents inconsistent VAAC boundaries. Akash's two dated cases (2026-09-25, 2026-10-07) are concrete examples; they are checked in another file.
- "often": not supported. No source gives a frequency. Either count cases from the archived feeds or drop the word.
- "dispatchers must act on the cautious one": inaccurate. The guidance says the operator decides through its SRA, treats VAAC, SIGMET and NOTAM information as the minimum or primary input, plans with other information it considers accurate and relevant, and should resolve conflicts (ICAO/EASA use "should"; this is guidance, not a hard rule). Caution is the default at the start of an eruption, not a rule to pick the most cautious product, and airlines visibly diverge.
- "quickly": supported (EASA's first actions; Doc 9974's "re-planning at short notice").

**Proposed wording (strongest the evidence supports):**

> When a volcano erupts, the airline decides what each of its flights does, not the ash advisory centre. ICAO and EASA guidance tells the operator to find which aircraft could be affected, alert the crews, re-plan departures, and resolve conflicts between the ash advisory, SIGMETs, NOTAMs, observatory notices and pilot reports. Those products come from different agencies on different clocks, and they do not always agree. TEPHRA does that first pass in minutes. It never shrinks an official area, it shows every disagreement with its source and time, and a duty dispatcher approves every action.

**Short version for a slide:** "Ash products come from different agencies at different times and can disagree. The rules make the airline sort that out, flight by flight, fast. TEPHRA drafts it; a dispatcher signs it."

**One proof line for the video** (pick one, keep the tag):
- "In July 2025, at least 24 international flights to and from Bali were cancelled while the airport said the ash had not affected Bali's own airspace." [CONFIRMED, The Nightly, 8 Jul 2025; both the count and the airspace statement come from the Ngurah Rai airport spokesperson] (Do not imply the airlines were wrong; ash on the routes can justify cancelling.)

## Verdict on Akash's point(s)

Grades unchanged after both 2026-10-10 verification passes; the reasons were tightened. The second pass strengthens D1 and D4 (Mulder et al. 2017 read in full) and adds a dated UK regulatory hook (AIC P 157/2025), but changes no grade.

- **D1, "Dispatchers may simply follow the most cautious official product (Doc 9974, SRA)": PARTLY.** Avoidance is the default at the onset of an eruption, and areas without concentration data count as "high" for UK-regulated operators. But Doc 9974 and EASA GM2 (formerly GM3) ORO.GEN.200(a)(3) give the operator discretion and say it should resolve conflicts between sources. They do not say "take the most cautious product." Doc 9974 para 2.10 advises CAAs not to stop operators who show in their SMS that they can fly near forecast ash safely. Airlines visibly diverge: Qantas and Virgin "at odds" in June 2011; Qantas delaying while Jetstar and Virgin cancelled on 7 July 2025. Mulder et al. (2017) independently state that the decision "is solely the responsibility of the airline operator, not the Civil Aviation Authority". The UK "treated as forecast high" rule is UK-only and under review for QVA (AIC P 157/2025). All of Doc 9974, EASA GM and SIB 2023-13 are guidance ("should"; the SIB says "Recommendations are not mandatory"), so do not call them mandates.
- **D2, "Lido and Jeppesen already draw official areas over routes": PARTLY.** Confirmed for Lido, but the SIGMET and VAA polygons are in the pilot-facing EFB (Lido Weather/mPilot). On the dispatch side, Lido Flight 4D builds volcanic ash into flight planning (NHM) and alerts on designated flights. Not found on Jeppesen's public pages. Weathernews goes further, with its own dispersion forecasts and affected-aircraft lists with ACARS uplink (turbulence only, so far).
- **D3, "Some disagreements are artefacts (lag, cautious envelope, VAAC not issued yet)": CONFIRMED in principle.** The NZ timing targets show the VONA, SIGMET and VAA arriving minutes apart, an initial SIGMET that may carry only the volcano name and location, and about 30 minutes with no forecast ash positions. IFALPA documented inconsistent VAAC boundaries in 2011 (historical; QVA is harmonising this). The guidance still makes the operator resolve these differences, so labelling each disagreement as lag, envelope or real conflict is itself the useful work. QVA adds one more product to reconcile from late 2026.
- **D4, "That dispatchers struggle is not verified": CONFIRMED (it is unverified).** A second, longer search also found no first-hand dispatcher complaint. The SITA workload claim is about weather in general, not ash. The nearest evidence is now confirmed from the full paper (Mulder et al. 2017): operations respondents, including flight planners, describe "so many operational decisions to be made in a short time" and fear extra information "would take too much time"; under conflicting forecasts their survey choices were poorly calibrated even though they say they only ever widen no-fly zones. That supports "disagreement under time pressure leads to inconsistent caution", not "dispatchers struggle".
- **D5, proposed wording: PARTLY.** "Differ" and "quickly" are supported. "Often" is unquantified, and "act on the cautious one" misstates the guidance. Use the wording proposed above, and use the 2011 Qantas/Virgin, 2025 Bali or Mulder proof line, not "Air New Zealand kept flying".
